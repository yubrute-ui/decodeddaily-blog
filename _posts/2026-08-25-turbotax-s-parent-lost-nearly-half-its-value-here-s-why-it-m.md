## When a Stock Falls 10× Harder Than Its Sector, Ask This One Question First

Intuit (INTU) has dropped roughly 43–45% over the past year (as of mid-2025), while the broad software ETF IGV lost just 4.4% over the same stretch (as of mid-2025). That's not noise. When a single stock falls more than ten times its sector benchmark, the market is sending a signal — but a signal about *what*, exactly, is the entire question.

This is where read-through analysis earns its keep.

---

## What Read-Through Analysis Actually Means

Read-through analysis is the practice of using one data point — a stock move, an earnings report, a competitor's commentary — to infer something about a *different* company, a sector, or a macro trend. It's a lateral thinking tool. You're not just asking "what does this mean for Intuit?" You're asking "what does the *divergence* between INTU and IGV tell me about how the market is pricing AI disruption risk in tax and financial software broadly?"

With INTU at roughly $357/share and a market cap around $97.78 billion (as of mid-2025), the stock's valuation metrics still lean cheap by historical standards — which creates a visible gap between price action and fundamental signals. That gap is the raw material for a read-through.

The core question isn't "should I buy INTU?" It's: **what is the market embedding in this price that the income statement hasn't confirmed yet?**

Possible answers: faster-than-expected AI commoditization of tax prep, competitive pressure from free IRS Direct File, or multiple compression across high-P/E software names. The read-through discipline forces you to *name the fear* before you can evaluate whether it's priced correctly.

---

## How to Spot a Read-Through Play in Any Sector

The pattern that makes INTU useful as a teaching case applies well beyond tax software. Here's the framework in three steps:

**1. Find the divergence, then classify it.**
A stock down 44% while its sector ETF is down 4% is a Category 1 divergence — far outside normal dispersion. But divergence alone tells you nothing directional. You need to classify it: is this company-specific (management, product failure, fraud risk) or is the market pricing a structural threat that could spread to peers?

If it's structural, the read-through flows *outward* to competitors. If INTU's drop is partly about AI replacing TurboTax-style guided software, then H&R Block (HRB) and any other UI-layer financial tool deserves the same scrutiny — regardless of whether their stocks have moved yet. Lagging peer stocks are often where the read-through trade lives.

**2. Check whether the sector ETF is lying.**
Sector ETFs can mask divergence. IGV down 4.4% sounds fine until you check its weighting. If the index is propped up by a handful of large-cap names with different exposure profiles, the "sector is fine" reading is misleading. Always decompose the ETF before concluding the sector is healthy. A flat IGV with INTU -44% might mean AI-exposed software is quietly getting repriced while infrastructure software holds the average up.

**3. Use the implied narrative as a stress test for your existing holdings.**
This is the most underused application. If the market's embedded narrative in INTU's drop is "AI commoditizes UI-layer software," stress-test every software position you hold against that thesis. Does your CRM exposure share the same risk? Does your payroll software holding? Read-through analysis isn't just about the stock in the headline — it's a prompt to audit your whole map.

---

## Is a Big Stock Drop a Buy or a Trap?

Neither label is useful without the read-through step. A 44% drop in a stock with intact fundamentals *could* be a valuation opportunity — but only if you've correctly identified why the market repriced it and concluded that fear is overblown or already fading.

The trap version looks identical from the outside: the valuation metrics still look cheap, the business still generates cash, the headlines feel sensational. The difference is whether the embedded fear is real and durable. AI replacing TurboTax is a legitimate structural threat. Whether it happens in two years or ten changes everything about whether $357/share (as of mid-2025) is cheap or just early-stage distress.

Read-through analysis won't resolve that uncertainty. What it does is force you to make the thesis explicit — so you know exactly what you're betting on and what evidence would prove you wrong.
