## Why a More Detailed Story Feels More True (Even When It Isn't)

Your brain is a storytelling machine. It craves narrative, coherence, and vivid detail. And most of the time, that's genuinely useful — stories help you remember, empathize, and make fast decisions. But this same storytelling instinct has a serious blind spot, one that trips up even highly educated, analytically minded people. It's called the **conjunction fallacy**, and once you understand it, you'll start noticing it everywhere.

---

## The Classic Example That Started It All

Imagine a woman named Linda. She's in her early thirties, single, outspoken, and deeply principled. As a student, she majored in philosophy, participated in anti-nuclear demonstrations, and was passionate about issues of social justice and equality.

Now answer this: Which is more probable?

- **A.** Linda is a bank teller.
- **B.** Linda is a bank teller who is active in the feminist movement.

If you gravitated toward option B, you're in very good company. In research by psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, the majority of participants — including statistically trained graduate students — chose the second option. And yet, by any standard rule of probability, that answer is *wrong*.

Here's why: Option B is a **subset** of Option A. Every feminist bank teller is, by definition, also a bank teller. That means the group described in B can never be larger than the group described in A. Adding a condition can only keep the probability the same or make it smaller — never bigger. The conjunction of two events is always less probable than either event alone.

---

## Why Your Brain Gets This So Wrong

The problem isn't intelligence. It's a cognitive shortcut called the **representativeness heuristic** — our tendency to judge probability based on how well something matches a mental prototype or story.

Linda's description *fits* the image of a feminist activist. The narrative clicks. So when you read "feminist bank teller," your brain doesn't calculate probability — it calculates *compatibility*. How well does this description match the story I just read? The answer feels obvious: very well. And that feeling of fit masquerades as a feeling of likelihood.

Detail doesn't increase the odds. It increases the sense of coherence. And coherence is deeply seductive to a pattern-seeking brain.

This is why conspiracy theories are often so persuasive. A vague claim — "something went wrong" — feels hollow. But a richly detailed narrative with named officials, specific dates, and plausible-sounding motives feels almost *too specific to be made up*. The specificity itself becomes evidence of truth in our minds, even though, logically, every additional claim added to the story makes the overall scenario *less* likely to be true.

---

## Where This Fallacy Shows Up in Real Life

The conjunction fallacy isn't just a quirky lab result — it surfaces in decisions that genuinely matter:

- **Investing and market predictions:** A detailed forecast about *why* a specific stock will rise under a specific set of conditions can sound far more convincing than a simple base-rate analysis — even when the simpler view is statistically stronger.
- **Medical diagnosis:** Patients (and sometimes clinicians) may find a detailed, specific diagnosis more believable than a broader, more probable one, simply because it fits their symptoms more neatly.
- **Legal reasoning:** Jurors can be swayed by a detailed, story-driven account of events even when the evidence for a more general conclusion is actually stronger.
- **News consumption:** Vivid, detailed narratives in headlines engage us more than statistical summaries — and we're more likely to believe them.

---

## How to Catch Yourself Before the Trap Closes

Awareness is a start, but it's rarely enough on its own. Here are a few concrete habits that can help:

1. **Ask the subset question.** Whenever you're evaluating a specific scenario, ask: *Is this a version of a broader situation?* If yes, the broader situation is always at least as probable.
2. **Separate "fits the story" from "is likely."** These feel the same but measure completely different things. Deliberately ask both questions out loud.
3. **Be suspicious of vivid detail.** The more colorful and specific a prediction or claim is, the more carefully you should scrutinize it — not less.
4. **Default to base rates.** Before getting drawn into narrative details, ask: *What's the general probability of this type of event happening at all?*

The conjunction fallacy is a reminder that our brains weren't built for probability — they were built for stories. Knowing the difference between a good story and a likely outcome might be one of the most underrated thinking skills you can develop.
