## The Gym You're Not Going To Is Still Charging You

You know exactly which membership it is. It's been sitting on your bank statement for months — maybe longer — and every time you scroll past it, you think *I'll deal with that later.* Not because you're busy. Not because you forgot. Because canceling requires a decision, and doing nothing requires absolutely nothing.

That's not laziness. That's your brain doing exactly what it was built to do.

---

## Why Canceling Feels Harder Than It Should

Here's what's actually happening under the hood: your brain doesn't experience "no change" as a neutral state. It registers the current situation — whatever you're already doing — as the baseline, and any departure from that baseline as a *risk.* Effort costs something. The status quo costs nothing upfront.

Psychologists call this **status quo bias** — the tendency to prefer things as they are, even when the alternatives would objectively serve you better. It's not about comfort or laziness. It's about how your mind weights options. The current option gets a thumb on the scale. Every alternative has to *earn* its place by overcoming that built-in advantage.

This is why you've reviewed your phone plan zero times in three years, even though you vaguely know you're probably overpaying. The switching math might be obvious. The switching *feeling* is not.

---

## Why Is It So Hard to Leave a Default — Even a Bad One?

Take Marcus. He's been on the same streaming bundle since his roommate moved out two years ago. Back then, they split it four ways. Now he's paying full price for three services he uses one of, and hasn't touched the other two since a specific show ended. He's not unaware of this. He's mentioned it to people. He just hasn't *done* anything about it.

The bundle is his default. Canceling two services feels like multiple micro-decisions: *What if I want to watch something on there? What if there's something good coming? What if I regret it?* Staying put feels like none of those questions exist yet.

Or consider performance reviews at work. Priya has a direct report who's been underperforming in the same specific way for seven months. She's noted it. She's thought about restructuring his responsibilities, reassigning certain tasks, having a real conversation about fit. But nothing has changed — because *changing something* requires her to own a decision, while letting it drift just... continues. The cost of inertia gets absorbed quietly. Until it doesn't.

Both Marcus and Priya are rational people making irrational non-choices. Status quo bias isn't about being uninformed. It's about what "doing nothing" feels like compared to doing *something.* Nothing feels safe. Something feels like exposure.

---

## How Do Businesses Actually Use This Against You?

Deliberately and well.

The free trial auto-converts because the company knows the cognitive gap between "cancel before it charges" and "actually cancel" is enormous. Insurance premiums quietly increase at renewal because reviewing the policy would require effort, and the existing policy is already *there*, already *running.* The default investment option in a 401(k) — whatever it is when you first sign up — tends to stay the default for years, because switching requires you to actively want something else enough to move.

None of this works through deception exactly. It works through architecture. The current option is always right there. Every alternative is a few steps away, and those steps cost something, and your brain has already voted for staying put before you've consciously weighed anything.

---

## What to Actually Do This Week

Once a month, open your bank statement and look at every recurring charge — every single one. For each item, ask yourself one question: *Would I actively sign up for this today, knowing what I know now?*

Not "is it worth it in theory." Not "maybe I'll use it eventually." Would you sign up for it *today.*

If you pause on the question, that pause is your answer. The hesitation is the bias losing its grip for a second — and that second is worth acting on before the default reasserts itself.

One charge. This week. That's the move.
