## You've Already Lost the Money. The Question Is How Much More You're About to Lose.

You opened the app to cancel the subscription. Hovered over the button. Then closed it — because you'd already paid for three months and hadn't really *used* it yet.

That moment? That's not discipline. That's a trap your brain built for you, and it has a name.

---

## Why Do We Keep Going Even When Everything Says Stop?

Escalation of commitment is what happens when you double down on a failing decision specifically *because* you've already invested in it. Not because the path ahead looks promising. Because the path behind feels too expensive to abandon.

The mechanism is almost elegant in how it works against you. When you've spent time, money, or energy on something, quitting doesn't just feel like quitting — it feels like *retroactively* making the original decision wrong. And your brain hates that. So instead of processing a bad outcome, it reframes doubling down as "protecting the investment." Sunk costs become a justification to keep going, even when every forward-looking signal screams otherwise.

This isn't a personality flaw. It's automatic. Researchers call it irrational escalation precisely because the past investment is economically irrelevant to what you should do *now* — but psychologically, it feels like the most relevant thing in the room.

---

## Why You Can't Quit That Job Even Though You're Miserable

Take Marcus. Three years at a company where he was passed over for promotion twice. He knows the culture won't change. His manager is checked out. But every time a recruiter slides into his LinkedIn DMs, he thinks: *I've already put in three years. I can't leave now.*

That logic sounds like loyalty. It's not. It's escalation of commitment in a suit.

The three years are gone regardless of whether Marcus stays or leaves tomorrow. They don't appreciate in value if he adds a fourth year. But because *leaving* feels like invalidating all that previous sacrifice, his brain reframes staying as the rational choice — even when it objectively isn't.

The painful truth: the only thing three years of sunk time actually buys you is a stronger psychological leash.

---

## Why Renovations, Relationships, and Side Projects All Feel Impossible to Abandon

Here's a different context. Priya is eight months into renovating a house she bought to flip. She's $40,000 over budget. The contractor just found a structural issue that'll cost another $18,000. Every week she doesn't sell, she's paying carrying costs.

But stopping feels unthinkable — because stopping means everything she already spent was "for nothing."

Except it wasn't for nothing. It was for a lesson, for data, for the current state of the house — whatever that's worth. The $40,000 doesn't disappear faster if she walks away. It already disappeared. The only variable left is how much more she stacks on top of it.

Casinos have known this forever. The longer someone sits at a table, the harder they'll fight to break even before leaving. The house doesn't need to cheat. Your own psychology keeps you seated. Every chip you lose becomes a reason to stay for one more hand.

---

## How to Actually Catch Yourself Before You Double Down Again

The standard advice — "ignore sunk costs" — is functionally useless. You can't just *decide* to override an automatic bias. You need to restructure the question.

Before you authorize another month, another check, another year, ask yourself this:

**"If I had invested nothing in this so far, would I start it today — with exactly the information I have right now?"**

Not "was it a good idea at the time?" Not "could it theoretically work?" Would you, with fresh eyes and zero history, choose to begin this?

If the answer is no — or even a long, uncomfortable pause — you have your answer. The past money is gone either way. The only question escalation of commitment refuses to let you ask clearly is: *how much more are you about to add?*

---

**This week, pick one thing you're continuing mainly because you've already invested in it.** Write down what you'd decide if you were starting from zero today. Don't analyze it. Just write the answer. Then decide whether your real reason for continuing can survive that question.
