## That Car You Test-Drove Is Now *Everywhere*

You spent Saturday afternoon at a dealership, sat in a slate-gray Honda CR-V, liked it, went home without buying. Sunday morning you're on the highway and you see one. Then another. Then three more at a single red light. By the time you reach the coffee shop, you're half-convinced Honda secretly flooded the market overnight.

They didn't. Those CR-Vs were always there. You just never had a reason to see them.

That's the Frequency Illusion — technically called the Baader-Meinhof phenomenon — and it's one of the quieter ways your brain rewrites your picture of reality without filing a disclosure.

---

## Why Does Everything Suddenly Feel Like It's Everywhere?

Here's what's actually happening under the hood.

Your brain is drowning in sensory input every second. To cope, it runs a filter — selective attention — that decides what gets promoted to conscious awareness and what stays in the background. Most of the time that filter is tuned to things that matter *to you right now*: your name spoken across a noisy room, the smell of food when you're hungry, a threatening shape in your peripheral vision.

When you encounter something new — a word, a car model, a person's name — your brain logs it as a fresh pattern worth tracking. The filter opens a new channel. Suddenly anything matching that pattern gets flagged and pulled forward. The world didn't fill up with that thing. Your attention architecture just started surfacing it.

The unsettling part: the brain doesn't announce this. It just delivers the result. You feel like you're observing a genuine uptick in frequency. That feeling is persuasive. It has the texture of evidence.

---

## How the Frequency Illusion Messes With Actual Decisions

**Scenario one — money.** You're scrolling Reddit on a Tuesday night and you first encounter the term "HELOC" (home equity line of credit). You'd never paid attention to it before. By Thursday you've seen it in two newsletters and overheard someone at work say it. By the weekend it feels like *everyone* is talking about HELOCs, which quietly creates the impression that this is a moment — that something is happening, that maybe you should look into it. The urgency feels market-driven. It's actually filter-driven. Whether a HELOC is right for you has nothing to do with how many times your newly-tuned brain flagged the word in 96 hours.

**Scenario two — health.** You read an article about early signs of a thyroid condition. It mentions fatigue, brain fog, sensitivity to cold. Suddenly you're noticing that you've actually been kind of tired lately. And now that you think about it, your focus has been off. And isn't it a little cold in here? Those symptoms existed before Monday. Your attention didn't. The article didn't give you a thyroid problem — it gave your brain a new search query to run against your existing experience.

Both scenarios share the same structure: a real feeling of increased frequency, driven entirely by a shift in your internal filter, mistaken for a shift in external reality.

---

## How to Tell If You're Inside a Frequency Illusion

One question does most of the work: *Did I encounter this concept, thing, or term for the first time recently?*

If the answer is yes — even if "recently" means last week — you're likely inside a Frequency Illusion. That doesn't mean the thing is irrelevant or wrong. It means the apparent surge in its presence is not independent evidence that it matters. You need to find a second source of signal that isn't routed through your newly opened attention filter.

This matters most when the illusion creates urgency. Financial products. Symptoms. Relationship patterns. Career moves. Any domain where "everyone seems to be doing this" can push you toward a decision — that's where the Frequency Illusion earns its damage.

---

## One Thing to Do Before You Act on a Pattern That Feels "Everywhere"

The next time something feels like it suddenly appeared in your life from every direction, open your notes app and write down: *when did I first hear about this?* Then give it two weeks before you let the perceived frequency influence a real decision. If it's still showing up — and you can point to sources that had nothing to do with your initial encounter — the pattern might be real. If it fades, you just watched your brain quietly close a filter it no longer needed.

The world didn't change. But now you know who did.
