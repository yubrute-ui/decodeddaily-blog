## That "We're the Same" Feeling Is Being Used Against You

You match with someone on Hinge. Within three messages, they've mentioned they're also from Austin, also hate corporate culture, also spent a summer in Southeast Asia. It feels like finding a rare frequency — someone who just *gets* it. You let your guard down faster than you normally would.

That ease you felt? Completely real. Whether it was earned? That's the question nobody asks.

This is in-group bias doing exactly what it was built to do — and someone else knew it would.

## Why Your Brain Fast-Tracks Trust for People Who Feel Like "Us"

In-group bias is the tendency to extend more trust, more generosity, and more forgiveness to people you categorize as part of your group — and less to everyone outside it. The grouping can be anything: hometown, college, sports team, political lean, dietary choices, the specific subreddit you both post in.

Your brain didn't develop this as a flaw. For most of human history, shared identity was a reliable signal. People in your tribe actually did share your interests, your risks, your stakes. Trusting them faster was efficient. Punishing in-group defectors harder kept the group intact. The whole system worked.

The problem is the shortcut hasn't updated, but the environment has. Shared identity can now be constructed by a stranger in under sixty seconds — from your Instagram bio, your Twitter likes, your LinkedIn headline. The *feeling* of "us" fires the same way whether the connection is twenty years old or twenty minutes old.

## Why Does Someone Mirroring Your Interests Feel So Disarming?

Because it's not just about similarity — it's about being *seen*. When someone reflects your interests back at you accurately, your brain interprets it as evidence they've been paying attention, that you matter to them. That interpretation triggers warmth and lowered skepticism before you've consciously decided to trust them.

Romance scammers don't open with a financial pitch. They spend days or weeks establishing overlap — your causes, your team, your city, your specific brand of humor. By the time the ask comes, the "us" feeling is so embedded that the request reads as coming from an insider, not a stranger. Cult recruiters run an almost identical sequence: rapid belonging establishment, genuine-feeling attunement, then the commitment ask. The sequence is the tell, not the similarity itself.

## How In-Group Bias Quietly Shapes Decisions at Work Too

It's not just predatory contexts. Sit in any hiring debrief and watch what happens when a candidate went to the same school as the hiring manager, or grew up in the same mid-size city, or used to work at a company the manager respects. The conversation shifts. Ambiguous answers get charitable reads. Gaps in experience get rationalized. Nobody announces they're doing it.

A manager interviews two equally qualified candidates. One dropped a reference to hiking the same trail system the manager posted about two weeks ago. The other didn't. The hiker gets the offer. The paper case the manager builds afterward — "better culture fit," "stronger communication" — is real in their mind. But the trail reference was the actual decision point.

This is in-group bias operating in slow motion, dressed as professional judgment.

## How to Catch It Before It Costs You

The diagnostic isn't "did we bond?" It's one specific question: **Did this connection exist before they needed something from me?**

If a coworker has been your friend for two years and now asks a favor, the shared history preceded the ask. That's a real relationship. If someone establishes common ground *right before* making a request — especially someone you just met — the sequence is backwards, and that inversion is worth noticing.

The "us" feeling will still fire. You can't stop that. But you can pause and ask whether the trust that feeling is handing over is actually proportional to the time and evidence you have.

## The One Thing to Try This Week

Next time someone establishes a point of connection right before asking you for something — a yes, a referral, a swipe, a signature — write down in your phone what you actually know about them from *before* that moment. Not what you felt. What you knew. If the list is short, the feeling is outrunning the data. Slow it down.

The "us" is real. The trust transfer is optional.
