## The GME Notification That Wouldn't Let You Sell

You bought GME at $28. It slid to $19. Then $14. Your brokerage app sends a little red badge — you dismiss it without opening. Not because you don't know what it says. Because opening it makes it real.

That's not a investing problem. That's your brain doing something very specific, and very predictable.

## Why does holding a losing stock feel better than selling it?

Here's what's actually happening when you refuse to close a position that every signal — the Smart Score, the analyst consensus, the price action — is screaming to exit.

Your brain isn't evaluating GME's next 12 months. It's doing something far more personal: it's trying to protect the version of you that bought in the first place.

This is the **sunk cost fallacy** in its purest form. The money you put in is gone. It left the moment the trade executed. Whether you sell today or hold for six more months doesn't change what you already spent — it only changes what happens *next*. But your brain refuses to process it that way. It treats the original purchase price like a debt the market owes you. Like holding long enough will eventually force reality to pay up.

It won't. The market has no memory of what you paid. GME's chart doesn't know your cost basis. The stock doesn't care.

## What happens when "I'll just wait for a recovery" becomes the whole strategy

Meet the two versions of this trap that actually play out in real life.

**Version one**: You bought GameStop shares during a quieter period — not the 2021 frenzy, just a regular trade based on a thesis that made sense at the time. The thesis is now clearly broken: the streaming pivot didn't materialize, the fundamentals didn't improve, the forecast models are pricing in underperformance. But you're still in. Why? Because selling at $14 when you bought at $28 means *you were wrong*. And being wrong costs more psychologically than the dollar loss does. So you convert a trading decision into an identity defense.

**Version two**: You're not even that emotionally attached to the stock — but you've told three people you were bullish on GME. You posted about it. Now exiting isn't just losing money. It's a public retraction. The sunk cost has jumped from financial to social. You're holding a position to avoid an awkward conversation that will probably never happen.

Both versions are the same mechanism. Past investment — money, reputation, ego — hijacks a decision that should only be about the future.

## Why does one bad trade keep pulling your attention all day?

Because your brain weighs losses roughly twice as heavily as equivalent gains. This isn't motivational-poster psychology — it's the documented asymmetry in how humans process outcomes. A $500 loss hits differently than a $500 gain feels good. So when GME is down, it doesn't sit quietly in your portfolio. It *intrudes*. Every market open, every Reddit thread, every "what's GME doing today" search is your brain trying to resolve something it can't resolve — because the only resolution it accepts is getting back to even.

That anchor price — what you paid — is doing enormous invisible work on your attention and your judgment.

## How do you know if you're holding for a reason or holding out of ego?

One question cuts through it cleanly.

*If I had zero position in GME right now — if I'd never touched this stock — would I open my app today and buy it at this price?*

Not "would I have bought it then." Today. At this price. With what you know now.

If the answer is no, you've already told yourself everything you need to know. You're not holding because GME is a good buy. You're holding because *you* bought it, and those are completely different reasons to own a stock.

---

**Here's the one thing to do before you close this tab**: Pull up whatever position you've been quietly ignoring. Ask that question out loud — actually say it. "Would I buy this right now?" The discomfort you feel in that two-second pause is the sunk cost fallacy doing its job. Notice it. Then decide based on tomorrow, not yesterday.
