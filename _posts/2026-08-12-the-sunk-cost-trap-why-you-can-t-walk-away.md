## You Kept Watching a Show You Hated for Four Seasons. Sound Familiar?

Season four. Forty episodes in. You're watching a character you stopped caring about two seasons ago make the same mistake for the eleventh time, and you're *still there* — blanket, snacks, the works.

Why? You don't even like this show anymore. But you've already spent twelve hours on it. The thought of stopping now feels like those twelve hours just... evaporate. So you keep going. Another episode. Another. Until you've finished a show that made you feel nothing, and you've added eight more hours to the pile.

That's not loyalty to a story. That's the sunk cost fallacy doing exactly what it does best — convincing you that what you've already lost should determine what you do next.

---

## Why Does Quitting Feel Like Losing, Even When Staying Is the Real Loss?

Here's the uncomfortable mechanic: your brain doesn't treat past investments as gone. It treats them as *collateral* — something you're still protecting by continuing. Psychologists call the underlying driver loss aversion. We feel the sting of a loss more sharply than the pleasure of an equivalent gain. So when you've already put in time, money, or effort, stopping feels like *confirming* a loss. Continuing feels like keeping the loss in limbo — unconfirmed, theoretically recoverable.

But the math doesn't work that way. What you've already spent is gone regardless of what you do next. The ticket was paid. The three weekends of work were spent. Whether you quit or continue, those resources don't return. The only thing your decision actually controls is what happens *from this moment forward*.

Your brain knows this abstractly. But it doesn't *feel* it. And feelings run decisions more than we'd like to admit.

## Real Situations Where Sunk Cost Is Running the Show

**The app subscription you haven't opened in three months.** You signed up for an annual plan on a productivity app — let's say Notion, Todoist, whatever it was — and you were sure this was going to change things. It didn't. But canceling now means "wasting" the remaining eight months you've already paid for. So you keep the tab open. You check it occasionally to justify keeping it. You don't cancel. The money is already gone either way.

**The job you've outgrown but can't leave.** You're two and a half years into a role that stopped teaching you anything around month eighteen. The salary is fine, the work is soul-flattening, and you've had three recruiters reach out in the past year. But you think: *I've built so much here. My manager trusts me. Starting over feels like erasing all of that.* So you stay. The two and a half years aren't going anywhere — they're on your resume either way. What you're actually choosing is the next two and a half years. And you're choosing them based on the ones that are already finished.

Notice the pattern: in both cases, the past investment *feels* like a reason. It isn't one. Feeling like a reason and being one aren't the same thing.

## How Do You Actually Catch Yourself Doing This?

The fallacy is slippery because "persistence" is genuinely a virtue in some contexts. Relationships take real work. Skills compound over years. Not every difficult stretch is a sign to exit.

The distinction is this: are you continuing because the *future* of this thing has value, or because the *past* of this thing demands tribute?

One honest question cuts through most of it — **"If I were starting fresh today, with no prior investment, would I still choose this?"**

Not "would I have done things differently." Not "was the investment worth it." Just: given where things actually stand right now, would you choose in?

If the honest answer is no, then what you're really doing is paying an ongoing tax on a sunk cost. Every additional hour, dollar, or unit of energy you put in is a new decision — and you get to make it fresh.

---

## The One Thing to Do Right Now

Pick one thing in your life where you've been telling yourself *"I've come too far to quit"* — a project, a subscription, a commitment, a creative endeavor — and write down, in one sentence, what it would give you over the *next six months* if you continued. Not what you've put in. Not what it meant before. What it actually delivers going forward.

If you can't write that sentence, you have your answer.
