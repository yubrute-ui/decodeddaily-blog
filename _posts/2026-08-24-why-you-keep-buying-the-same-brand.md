## That Brand You Always Grab? You've Never Actually Chosen It.

You're standing in the toothpaste aisle at CVS. There are eleven options. You spend maybe four seconds, grab the blue Colgate box, and move on. You didn't read labels. You didn't compare fluoride percentages. You just… reached.

And you'd swear it's your preference.

This is the Mere Exposure Effect in action — the psychological phenomenon where repeated exposure to something, all by itself, makes you like it more. Not because it's better. Not because you consciously approved it. Because your brain has seen it before, and familiarity quietly registers as *good*.

Psychologist Robert Zajonc first demonstrated this formally in the 1960s, showing that people rated stimuli they'd been exposed to more frequently as more pleasant — even when they couldn't remember seeing them before. The scary part isn't that it happens. It's that it happens *below* your awareness.

---

## Why Does a Song You Hated Start to Sound Good After a Week?

Think about the last time a track came on that you immediately dismissed. Too weird, too repetitive, whatever. Then it kept showing up — your roommate's playlist, the coffee shop, a TikTok you scrolled past. Three weeks later, you catch yourself humming it.

Nothing changed about the song. What changed is that your brain stopped spending energy processing it as *unfamiliar*. Unfamiliar things require cognitive work. Familiar things feel smooth, frictionless — and that smoothness gets misread as *liking*. Researchers call this processing fluency: when something is easy to mentally process, that ease carries a faint positive charge.

This is why radio stations have always hammered the same fifteen songs. Why Netflix autoplays the next episode. Why Spotify's algorithm walks you in slow circles. The product doesn't get better — it gets *easier*, and easier feels like better.

## How This Is Quietly Steering Your Professional Opinions Too

Here's where it gets less comfortable. The Mere Exposure Effect doesn't just apply to toothpaste and pop songs. It applies to *people*.

Picture your office Slack. There's a colleague — call her Priya — who's always the first to comment on project threads, always in the all-hands, always dropping a quick reaction on the CEO's posts. She's visible. Constantly, low-stakes visible. Come performance review season, her name feels familiar in a way that carries warmth. You'd frame it as "she's always engaged." But part of what you're feeling is pure exposure.

Meanwhile, someone who does excellent, quiet work but rarely appears in the performative spaces of your team's communication? They feel hazier. Harder to immediately champion. Not because their work is worse — because your brain hasn't been warmed up by repetition.

This is why "visibility" advice in career development isn't just about self-promotion optics. It's about how human brains actually process merit — imperfectly, with a thumb on the scale for whoever showed up most.

---

## Does Familiarity Always Mean You're Being Fooled?

Not exactly. Familiarity can be a reasonable heuristic in low-stakes, low-information situations. If you've used a brand of olive oil a dozen times without incident, defaulting to it saves cognitive energy you can spend elsewhere. The exposure effect isn't always working against you.

The problem is that your brain applies the *same* heuristic regardless of stakes. It nudges you toward the familiar candidate on a ballot the same way it nudges you toward the familiar granola bar at Trader Joe's. The warmth feels identical whether the decision matters enormously or barely at all.

That's the trap: the feeling doesn't scale with importance. You have to do that scaling manually.

---

## Try This Before Your Next "Obvious" Choice

The next time you're about to default to something — a vendor your team always uses, an app you recommend without thinking, a person you instinctively trust in a meeting — pause and ask one question:

**"Have I actually evaluated this, or have I just seen it a lot?"**

You don't need to second-guess everything. You need to notice when familiarity is doing the choosing *instead of* you. Name the pull. That two-second pause is usually enough to crack open the difference between a real preference and an exposure hangover wearing preference's clothes.

Which one are most of yours?
