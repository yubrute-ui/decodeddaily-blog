## The Supplement Bottle With 47 Ingredients Probably Doesn't Work Better

You're staring at two protein powders. One has five ingredients. The other has a "proprietary matrix" of forty-seven compounds, a chart with arrows pointing at arrows, and a section titled *"Synergistic Bio-Optimization Complex."* You don't fully understand what half of it means. But you put the second one in your cart anyway.

That's not you being irrational. That's complexity bias doing exactly what it's supposed to do — making the complicated option *feel* more credible, without requiring you to actually evaluate it.

Here's how the shortcut runs in your brain: effort signals value. If someone built something intricate, they must have thought harder about it. More layers mean more expertise. Simple things feel like they're *missing something*. So when two options sit side by side, the dense one wins by default — not because it's better, but because it *looks* like more work went into it.

---

## Why does a complicated explanation feel more trustworthy than a simple one?

The bias isn't random. It has a logical-ish root. In most of your lived experience, expertise *does* come with complexity. A surgeon explains your procedure with terminology you don't recognize. A tax accountant talks about depreciation schedules and basis adjustments. Over time, your brain learns: "when I don't fully understand something, that's often because the person knows more than me."

That's a reasonable heuristic — until marketers, agencies, and product designers start manufacturing complexity specifically to trigger it.

A software pricing page is a clean example. Companies like project management tools or CRM platforms often bury their most functional mid-tier plan under five or six columns of checkmarks and feature names you'd have to research to understand. The cheapest plan is always visually cramped. The enterprise plan glows. The comparison is technically there — but the cognitive load of parsing it steers you toward "just get the better one" almost by fatigue alone. The complexity isn't accidental. It makes comparison harder and perceived value higher, simultaneously.

Same thing happens in marketing proposals. An agency pitches you with a 40-slide deck covering "multi-channel attribution modeling," "sentiment velocity metrics," and a proprietary framework with a four-letter acronym. Another agency says: "We'll run two ad channels, test copy every two weeks, cut what doesn't convert." The second pitch sounds thin. It probably isn't — but it *feels* undercooked next to the jargon mountain.

## How do I know if complexity is actually solving something — or just obscuring it?

This is the question that cuts through the bias, and it requires you to briefly ignore how something *feels* and ask what the complexity is *doing*.

Real complexity earns its place. A clinical trial protocol is dense because it needs to control for dozens of variables that actually affect the outcome. A legal contract is long because ambiguity in certain clauses has historically cost people money in court. The complexity maps onto real problems.

Manufactured complexity doesn't map onto anything — it's decorative. That supplement with 47 ingredients? Most of the compounds at those doses aren't doing measurable work. The extra ingredients are there because a bottle that says "47 active compounds" charges more than one that says "creatine, protein, electrolytes." The complexity is the product.

The tell is usually this: when you ask someone to explain *why* each layer exists, they either can't, or they generate more jargon to cover the gap. Genuine expertise can almost always simplify on request. Manufactured complexity collapses when you press it.

---

## What to actually do when you feel confused by something you're about to buy or decide on

Don't immediately trust the confusion as evidence of depth. Confusion is neutral — it just means information didn't transfer cleanly. The question is *why* it didn't transfer.

Before you commit, try this once: ask for the one-sentence version. Not dismissively — genuinely. "Can you explain the core mechanism in plain language?" If the answer sharpens your understanding, the complexity probably has substance behind it. If the answer just restates the jargon with different jargon, you've found your answer.

That single test — ask for the plain version — won't make you a cynic about complexity. It just makes you someone who requires complexity to justify itself before you pay for it.

That's a cheaper education than the 47-ingredient protein powder.
